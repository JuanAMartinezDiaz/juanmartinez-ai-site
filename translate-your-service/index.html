The Work Did Not Become Smaller When the Uniform Came Off

Why military experience is difficult to translate, and how Mission 16 helps Veterans build a civilian career record they can defend.

By Juan A. Martinez Diaz, MBA · August 28, 2026

A record written for a different reader

A military evaluation can describe a year of demanding work and still leave a civilian recruiter unsure what the person knows how to do. Military records support assignments, readiness, advancement, awards, and other service decisions. Civilian employers are reading for responsibility, leadership scope, technical capability, judgment, results, and relevance to the position they need to fill.

That difference can hide valuable experience. A Veteran may have led people across several locations, protected systems supporting a large population, recovered a failing operation, or managed scarce resources under pressure. If the résumé says only “served as operations NCO,” the employer does not receive enough information to evaluate the work.

I had to make this translation myself

When I retired from the Army, I carried the experience of a Sergeant Major. I understood the work I had done. I also learned that civilian employers did not share the vocabulary the Army used to describe it.

The answer was not to replace military terms with larger corporate titles. Calling a senior noncommissioned officer a vice president does not explain the work and may create a claim the person cannot defend. I had to separate rank from responsibility and describe what had actually been entrusted to me: people, technology, readiness, cybersecurity, continuity, geographic reach, and the consequences attached to failure.

The resources exist, but the path is difficult to assemble

The Department of Defense provides the Verification of Military Experience and Training record. O*NET maintains a Military Crosswalk that connects occupation codes with civilian fields. Service credentialing programs identify related credentials, while the Department of Veterans Affairs provides career counseling, résumé support, and employment assistance.

Each resource can contribute evidence or context. None can know which duties the individual performed, what decisions were made, what changed because of the work, or what the person wants to do next.

The occupation provides context. The Veteran provides the evidence.

Mission 16 begins with the person

Two people can hold the same occupation code and leave the service with different experience. Their units, assignments, collateral duties, equipment, decisions, and leadership scope may have little in common.

Translate Your Service asks about work personally performed before drafting a résumé. It records the original military language, the Veteran’s explanation, the proposed civilian interpretation, the supporting evidence, and anything that remains unresolved. Public sources help explain occupational context; they are not used to manufacture personal experience.

Clear translation does not prove immediate readiness

A strong translation may show that a service member has valuable experience adjacent to a civilian role. It does not establish that every requirement has already been performed in the same environment.

Mission 16 separates direct experience, transferable strengths, evidence gaps, development needs, demonstrated learning agility, present readiness, likely learning curve, and potential. A hiring manager or fellowship host should be able to see what the participant can contribute now and what support would still be needed.

Fellowship matching requires a different record

Telisse Tess Rodriguez challenged the first design from the perspective of someone who works directly with military recruiting fellowships. Her point was practical: a translated résumé may improve understanding, but it does not determine whether a participant and host assignment are a sound mutual fit.

The revised mission adds a one-page Fellowship Match Profile, an accomplishment bank, and a host-manager brief. They capture target role families, evidence-backed capabilities, development priorities, work environment, placement constraints, present readiness, learning curve, and the support a host would need to provide.

We tested this layer with separate synthetic Army and Air Force cases. The Airman case scored 98 out of 100 with no critical failures. That is enough to move the design into a limited, de-identified human pilot. It is not evidence of broad validation, and I will not present it that way.

Career fit belongs to the participant

Military members have often had limited control over assignments, schedules, locations, and operating conditions. Civilian transition introduces choices that may be unfamiliar. Mission 16 helps the participant identify essential work needs, nonnegotiables, practical constraints, and the kind of work likely to remain sustainable.

Those answers guide the participant’s decisions. They are not résumé claims and should not be shared with an employer without approval. Family considerations should be translated into the condition that matters, such as travel or schedule limits, without collecting unnecessary personal details.

What Mission 16 produces

The Military-to-Civilian Translation Packet includes an evidence record, terminology crosswalk, two or three career directions, a civilian-ready résumé or CV appropriate to the participant’s target level, a targeted version, an accomplishment bank, and a cover letter when useful or required.

It also provides a LinkedIn foundation, an unsupported-claim report, a private Career Fit Profile, a Role Readiness and Development Profile, fellowship materials when applicable, and a handoff into Mission 15. Mission 15 can then professionalize the approved LinkedIn record, evaluate aligned roles, and support controlled applications without repeating the translation work.

The Veteran controls the translation

No agent should enlarge military service simply because a stronger claim sounds marketable. It should not invent team size, budgets, credentials, deployments, combat history, clearance status, technical knowledge, or measurable results. Unresolved claims remain outside final career documents.

The same discipline protects privacy. Social Security numbers, medical information, home addresses, protected operational details, and security information that should not be disclosed do not belong in the prompt or the finished packet.

This mission is personal to me

I know what the military asks of its people. I also know how strange it can feel to leave an institution that understood your rank, history, and responsibilities and enter a market that sees a résumé before it sees you.

Veterans should not have to diminish their service to appear relevant. They should not have to inflate it either. They need a fair translation of what they were trusted to do and evidence they can stand behind when the interview begins.

The uniform may come off. The work, judgment, discipline, and responsibility developed through service do not disappear with it.
